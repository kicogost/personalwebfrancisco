I once had the CV I'd been told to want. Management consulting in London, the
right logos in the right order. On paper I'd made it. Then one night I woke up
soaked in sweat, sure I was having a heart attack. I wasn't. My body was
telling me something my calendar had spent a year ignoring.

I changed almost everything in the months after. Left what needed leaving.
Started stretching every morning, sitting still for ten minutes, getting choked
by strangers three times a week. None of it was complicated; the only hard part
was doing it every single day. Within a few months I was living a different
life, and I've been building on that ground ever since.

Three things stuck.

Hard work is still the only thing that works, and it's never been harder to
actually do. AI will get you to okay on anything, instantly. Your phone will
pay you in dopamine for doing nothing at all. Both are useful, and both are
quietly expensive. Becoming someone who can still work hard, in an age
engineered to make that feel unnecessary, is a rare advantage that compounds.
So that's what I train for.

Systems beat one-off outputs. The weeks I'm proudest of are the boring ones,
where the thing ran without me having to save it. Anyone can pull off a result
once; the real work is building the machine that produces it every time.

Real work is the content. I don't write about things I haven't done, so
everything I publish is downstream of something I shipped, broke, or paid for
out of my own pocket.

The rest is simpler than people make it. Be kind to yourself first, then your
body, then everyone else. Hold your ideals firmly, and change them the moment
someone gives you a good reason to. Fight when it's time to fight, forgive when
it's time to forgive. Say please, thank you, and I'm sorry.

Marcus Aurelius put it better than I can: stop arguing about what a good man
should be, and be one.
